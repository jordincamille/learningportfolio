Before I could write a single word about why I wanted to study in Germany, I had to find out whether the plan actually worked. An Indonesian diploma isn't directly recognized there, so the real first question wasn't "which university"; it was "which bridge gets me to one."

Why Data Analytics, Why Germany

Matches my strengths (statistics, coding, business acumen) and is in demand across every industry.

Public universities are tuition-free; a semester fee of roughly €300–400 is the only cost.

A strong engineering & industrial economy, with SAP, Siemens, and Deutsche Bahn, plus a growing Berlin tech scene.

A large international student community: TU Berlin alone hosts students from 130+ nationalities.

“Inquisitive. You ask a lot of questions,” Ms. Kier answered after two minutes of scouring for an adjective that best described me; as she predicted about me, I subconsciously asked her more questions: “Is that a good thing? What does inquisitive mean exactly?”

I counted the number of syllables in the word. Four, a terribly unlucky number in Chinese culture, because I hoped that it would be five-syllables-long to match my lucky number and identified the etymology of inquisitive (prefix in- meaning in; -quis- meaning to seek; suffix -tive meaning tendency) to realize that the word means a tendency to seek. Words like “curious” and “knowledge-seeking” in Ms. Kier’s definition substantiated my etymological epiphany, and I sensed inquisitive’s positive connotation but wondered: my questions are a good thing?

Asking questions and actively participating in “adult conversations” are acts that diu lian [lose face] and bring shame. I grew up being instructed to stare at the shriveling cabbage in the hot-pot broth rather than to converse with family friends about their unfamiliar lives. I never found just eating fulfilling but gaining answers to questions, like “Where did you get that ring?” “How did you meet my mother?” “What happened when you first came to Jakarta?”- satiating. Everyone at the dinner table was a book to decode with anecdotes that may foreshadow my future, teach me a lesson, or make for an incredibly amusing story.

However, when it was decided that I lost too much face, I was instructed to complete math workbooks. Learning multiplication in the first-grade, a year before I was supposed to learn it in school, and realizing that five-times-five makes a number too big to count on my hands shattered my confidence. When my questions about the multiplication table were met with disappointment, questions became a symbol of stupidity.

The fear of losing face puppeteered me with strings of anxiety and shame of asking a “useless question” or giving a wrong answer in front of the whole class. I retreated into silence and suppressed my questions for years until I sat in Ms. Kier’s classroom.

Like a moth flying towards a light bulb, I desperately wanted to know the answers behind each of Ms. Kier’s guided questions in her English class; she always had an illuminating answer about literature, philosophy, and history that my classmates and I had to guess at, asking even more questions. The Socratic seminars in class facilitated a community that helped me shed my fears of losing face; so much that on most days Ms. Kier could not call on me anymore because I had answered too much, but I attended her office-hours after class to ask about potential literary foils, allusions, symbols, and ironic situations found in classic books that I read outside of class (my favorite being John Steinbeck's East of Eden).

Questions are catalysts of knowledge, so I hope that sharing my story highlights the importance of inquisitiveness (a five-syllable word, matching my lucky number), rejects the notion that asking questions loses face, rouses laughter, and pays tribute to whom I will forever ask: did you know that there is no i in inquisitive without u?

This ends on the office-hours scene: a great close for a story about a classroom, but it never says what any of this has to do with university, or with data.

Full Final Text

FINAL

“Inquisitive. You ask a lot of questions,” Ms. Kier answered after two minutes of searching for the adjective that best described me. As she predicted, I subconsciously asked her more questions: Is that a good thing? What does inquisitive mean exactly?

I counted its syllables: four, a terribly unlucky number in Chinese culture. I wished it were five, my lucky number. Then I dissected the word itself: the prefix in-, the root -quis- meaning “to seek,” and the suffix -tive, suggesting a tendency. Inquisitive: a tendency to seek. Words like “curious” and “knowledge-seeking” substantiated Ms. Kier’s definition, and I sensed the word’s positive connotation. Still, I wondered: Are my questions a good thing?

Growing up, asking questions and actively participating in “adult conversations” were acts that diu lian [lose face] and bring shame. I was instructed to stare at the shriveling cabbage in the hot-pot broth rather than converse with family friends about their unfamiliar lives. Yet eating alone never fulfilled me. I wanted answers to questions like, Where did you get that ring? How did you meet my mother? What happened when you first came to Jakarta? Everyone at the dinner table became a book waiting to be decoded, their anecdotes capable of foreshadowing my future, teaching me a lesson, or becoming an amusing story.

When my questions were instead redirected toward math workbooks, however, curiosity became something I distrusted. In first grade, I learned multiplication a year before I was supposed to encounter it in school. Realizing that five times five was already too large to count on my hands shattered my confidence. When my questions about the multiplication table were met with disappointment, I began associating questions with stupidity. The fear of losing face puppeteered me with strings of anxiety and shame: What if I asked a useless question? What if my answer was wrong? I retreated into silence and suppressed my questions for years.

Then I sat in Ms. Kier’s classroom.

In her English class, I desperately wanted to uncover the answers behind her guided questions. Her lessons on literature, philosophy, and history rarely ended with one definitive answer; instead, they created more questions. Through Socratic seminars, I gradually learned that uncertainty did not make me unintelligent, it gave me something to investigate. I became comfortable enough to contribute repeatedly in class, and eventually my curiosity extended beyond what was required. During office hours, I asked about literary foils, allusions, symbols, and irony in the classic books I read independently, especially John Steinbeck’s East of Eden. More importantly, I learned that asking a question was not an admission that I lacked knowledge. It was evidence that I wanted to gain more.

That change transformed the questions I wanted to ask. I became interested not only in what people choose, but why they choose it: why someone might value an immediate reward over a greater future one, why the same information can lead two people to make different decisions, or why our choices sometimes contradict what we believe is best for ourselves. Eventually, I became interested in whether patterns could be found behind these choices. Instead of only asking why people behave differently, I wanted to understand how their decisions could be examined through evidence and data. My curiosity was beginning to move from asking questions to finding ways to investigate them.

This is what draws me to Wirtschaftswissenschaften at Universität Ulm. I am particularly interested in the program's combination of economics with Data Science and AI, because it would allow me to understand economic decision-making while developing the quantitative tools to analyze it. I am especially drawn to the opportunity to build a Business Analytics profile, where I can learn how data is stored, processed, and analyzed to support better decisions. The program's emphasis on quantitative methods also appeals to me because I want to strengthen the mathematical and statistical thinking behind my curiosity. Courses and opportunities involving areas such as Data Analytics, Machine Learning and Decision Making, and empirical analysis would allow me to move from simply asking questions to testing them.

In the future, I hope to build a career in data analysis and business analytics, using statistics and data-driven methods to understand patterns, evaluate decisions, and solve real-world problems. What attracts me to this field is not simply working with numbers; it is what those numbers allow me to discover. I want to be the person who notices a pattern, asks why it exists, and has the analytical skills to investigate the answer. Universität Ulm’s combination of economics, quantitative methods, Data Science, and Business Analytics would give me the foundation to turn that habit of questioning into a practical skill.

Questions have been catalysts for my knowledge, but more importantly, they have become a measure of how much I am willing to understand. I no longer see asking as a way of admitting what I do not know. I see it as the beginning of finding out. At university, I am ready to question assumptions, investigate patterns, and learn from answers that challenge my own. I began by asking whether being inquisitive was a good thing. I now know that the more important question is what I choose to do with that curiosity.
